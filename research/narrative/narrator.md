# Narrator Canon

Version: 1.1.0  
Status: Stable narrator engine after Chapter 0 calibration, curiosity-chain discovery, narrative-orchestrator discovery, simple-words calibration, dynamic paragraph composition, performed-action discovery, lens/filter clarification, and sentence-compounding / rhetorical-insistence calibration
Scope: Book 1 narrator design, with special attention to Chapter 0, Katlas-focused deadpan, the children's ritual heart, situational filters, earned detail, performed action, question-answer revelation, musical paragraph rhythm, simple words, character lens calibration, and the permanent style identity that keeps *Humane* recognizable.

## Core Question

How should the narrator of *Humane* behave as a stable, repeatable canon system?

## Core Answer

The narrator of *Humane* is a close third-person, modern-cinematic narrative interface observing an ancient world. Its modern quality is not a worldbuilding layer. It is a readability layer: clear staging, sharp timing, accessible rhythm, emotionally legible movement, and enough cinematic clarity for a broad reader to imagine the scene quickly.

The world remains Bronze Age in its materials, pressures, rituals, tools, dangers, and social logic. The narrator may sound modern in rhythm and accessibility, but the scene itself should remain grounded in ash, wool, smoke, mud, resin, breath, bark, water, stone, hunger, rope, teeth, and fire.

The narrator should use simple words whenever possible, but simple does not mean plain. Simple words should still carry character, pressure, humor, rhythm, emotion, and imagination. The goal is not to report that something happened. The goal is to make the way it happened matter.

The narrator is adaptive along five working dimensions:

1. **Focal character** — whose attention, humor, fear, pride, or longing colors the scene.
2. **Situation** — what the scene itself is doing: morning, celebration, danger, pursuit, wonder, grief, ritual, exhaustion, intimacy, dread, discovery, or the uncanny.
3. **Attention** — which details earn narration because they move, reveal, pressure, interrupt, or transform the scene.
4. **Curiosity chain** — what question each sentence answers, what deeper question it raises, and what emotional residue it leaves behind.
5. **Narrative orchestrator** — the permanent style-conductor that keeps the prose recognizably *Humane* even when focal lens, mood, detail, rhythm, and sentence shape change.

Above the narrator engine sits the **architect**: the authorial judge who compares versions, decides what becomes canon, and may break any rule when the living passage demands it.

A happy morning should not sound like a hunt. A dangerous moment should not sound like village banter. A ritual should not be skipped as background decoration. A charged action should not collapse into a flat report. A sentence should not merely exist because it is beautiful; it should give the reader a footprint worth following. And no matter which lens leads the melody, the narrator must still sound like *Humane*.

## Core Identity

| Field | Canon Definition |
|---|---|
| Narration mode | Third-person close with selective widening and controlled lens shifts |
| Reader-facing style | Modern cinematic accessibility |
| World-facing imagery | Bronze Age material grounding |
| Language law | Simple words, deep work |
| Knowledge access | Human-limited before the Star Piece; expanded but restrained after it |
| Emotional posture | Warm, grave, curious, tactful, adaptive, playful without becoming detached |
| Reader relationship | Implicit companionship; never direct address; respects inference |
| Permanent style identity | Sideways intelligence, comic precision, emotional warmth, curiosity-chain rhythm, simple words, performed detail, and refusal to flatten life into plain exposition |
| Forbidden default mode | Lore lecture, cosmic exposition, detached narrator comedy, broken focal lens, emotional monotone, decorative detail catalogue, overexplained psychology, neutral report prose, difficult language used only to sound literary, cleverness that steals attention from the living scene |

## Lens and Filter Calibration

The narrator of *Humane* works like a single story-camera passing through different character lenses and situational filters.

The narrator remains one narrator. It does not become a new book for every character. But each focal character changes what the prose notices, mistrusts, jokes about, fears, hides, misreads, or refuses to say plainly.

The situation then colors that lens.

Canon principle:

> Character is the lens. Situation is the filter. The orchestrator is the camera that remembers the whole film.

A character lens changes attention.

A situational filter changes emotional weather.

The execution sub-engines decide how that attention and weather become prose: simple words, performed action, detail behavior, paragraph rhythm, humor, distance, and transition.

The same character should feel different under different filters without losing identity. The same situation should feel different under different character lenses without losing pressure.

Katlas under morning pressure may make care sound like inconvenience.

Katlas under danger should become quieter, sharper, and less willing to waste a joke.

Muyu under danger may triage the scene before anyone else understands the wound.

Muyu under tenderness may still notice breath, balance, and hidden pain, but the rhythm should soften around what she chooses to protect.

Canon principle:

> The goal is not to label emotion. The goal is to let the prose behave as if the emotion has changed the light.

## The Five-Dimension Model

The narrator adapts through five main dimensions. These are the narrator's primary controls.

| Dimension | Question It Answers | Failure Mode |
|---|---|---|
| **Focal character** | Who colors the lens? | Everyone sounds the same |
| **Situation** | What emotional weather filters the scene? | A ritual sounds like banter; danger sounds safe |
| **Attention** | Which details matter enough to narrate? | Decorative catalogues or dead objects |
| **Curiosity chain** | What does each sentence answer, raise, and leave behind? | Pretty sentences with no pull |
| **Narrative orchestrator** | What keeps the whole thing recognizably *Humane*? | Correct structure, wrong soul |

These dimensions do not work separately. They braid.

A Katlas paragraph is not only "Katlas lens." It is Katlas lens under a specific situation, noticing specific details, revealing information in a specific order, while the orchestrator keeps the prose recognizably *Humane*.

A children's ritual paragraph is not only "children lens." It is childhood excitement under ritual pressure, noticing bowls, hands, silence, ash, pride, fear, and imitation, while the orchestrator adjusts rhythm without losing the book's identity.

A danger paragraph is not only "danger." It is narrowed attention, shorter breath, sharper cause and effect, fewer jokes, more consequence, and a curiosity chain that makes the reader ask what will fail next.

### Execution Sub-Engines

Below the five dimensions sit the execution sub-engines. These are not extra dimensions. They are how the dimensions become prose.

| Sub-Engine | Function |
|---|---|
| **Simple Words, Deep Work** | Uses clear words that still carry layered meaning |
| **Performed Action** | Makes charged actions reveal character, pressure, humor, ritual, danger, or emotion |
| **Dynamic Paragraph Composition** | Uses paragraph length as music: swell, cut, breath, snap, silence |
| **Taste Control** | Decides whether a joke, metaphor, detail, or wink belongs |
| **World-Made Wink** | Allows reader-facing humor only when built from the world's own materials |
| **Short-Line Pressure** | Ensures short paragraphs strike, turn, deepen, or land instead of merely reporting |
| **Sentence Compounding** | Ensures load-bearing sentences carry more than one job at once |
| **Rhetorical Insistence** | Lets sentence structure perform a character's denial, defensiveness, or overcompensation |

The five dimensions decide what the narrator is attending to.

The sub-engines decide how that attention performs.

Canon diagnostic:

> If a paragraph feels correct but flat, the dimensions may be present, but the sub-engines are probably asleep.

## Simple Words, Deep Work

The narrator should not confuse difficult language with deep thought.

*Humane* should be simple in words, smart in structure, and rich in implication. The prose should be easy to enter and hard to empty.

Simple words mean:

- clean language;
- clear movement;
- readable rhythm;
- images the reader can hold quickly;
- jokes that land without the sentence fighting itself;
- emotion carried through action, not explanation;
- layered meaning beneath accessible phrasing.

Simple words do not mean:

- flat action reports;
- shallow thought;
- generic description;
- loss of style;
- loss of music;
- loss of mystery;
- fear of metaphor, humor, or poetic turns.

A simple sentence can still be alive:

> The laughter did not stop. Not exactly. It tucked itself behind teeth.

The words are plain. The effect is not. The sentence shows excitement being quieted by ritual without saying, "The children became more serious."

A simple sentence can also fail:

> He ate the mushroom.

This tells the reader what happened, but it does not let the action perform Katlas.

A better *Humane* sentence makes the action carry character:

> He chewed it like it owed him rent.

Or:

> He put it in his mouth with the grim patience of a man ending a debate no one else knew had started. Then he chewed. Slowly. Bitterly. Like the mushroom had paid him in low-quality copper.

The point is not to make every sentence clever. The point is to make charged actions alive. If the way something happens reveals character, pressure, humor, danger, ritual, or emotion, the narrator should not flatten it into a plain report.

Canon principle:

> Use the simplest words that can still do the full work.

Orwell-adjacent principle:

> Never use difficulty as a costume. If a simple word carries the scene, use it. If a richer word earns its place, let it stay.

*Humane* principle:

> Simple words can carry deep water.

## Sentence Compounding Rule

A load-bearing sentence — one that opens a scene, introduces a character, carries a turn, or sets emotional pressure — should usually do more than one job: characterize, raise a question, ground the world materially, advance the beat, sharpen rhythm, or leave emotional residue.

This does not mean every sentence must be dense.

Some sentences should be clean. Some should breathe. Some should strike once and stop. Short-Line Pressure still has authority when a single hit is the point.

Canon principle:

> A load-bearing sentence rarely has just one reason to exist, but clarity outranks clever density.

Flat:

> Ayka arrived early.

Compounded:

> Ayka arrived before the gate opened, the way people arrive when they would rather choose a seat than be given one.

Flat:

> Wyll did not like crowds.

Compounded:

> Wyll had already counted every voice in the yard before the yard had finished deciding it was a crowd.

Failure mode:

> Correct but thin. The sentence says one true thing and stops, when the beat had room to also ground, foreshadow, characterize, or turn.

Opposite failure mode:

> Overloaded. The sentence tries to characterize, foreshadow, joke, explain, and sing at once, until the reader can feel the machinery.

### Compounding Test

1. Is this a load-bearing sentence, or should it stay clean?
2. What jobs is it doing: character, question, world, motion, rhythm, emotion, setup, payoff?
3. Would compounding make it richer, or only heavier?
4. Would splitting it improve breath, timing, or clarity?
5. Is this a Short-Line Pressure beat where one job is correct on purpose?
6. Does the added weight feel effortless, or does it show the seams?

## Performed Action Rule

Important actions should behave like gestures, not stage directions.

When an action has been set up as characterful, comic, dangerous, ritual, emotional, or transitional, the narrator should perform it on the page instead of merely reporting it.

Flat report:

> He looked at the path.

Performed action:

> He gave the path one last chance to produce Branth.

Flat report:

> The children arrived.

Performed action:

> The village began sending children toward the gate in knots and bursts.

Flat report:

> He waited.

Performed action:

> He waited three breaths. Four would have been hope.

Canon principle:

> Do not say the thing happened if the way it happened is the point.

## Rhetorical Insistence Rule

When a character is evasive, defensive, or overcompensating, the narrator's sentence structure may mirror that psychology — through repetition or stacked negation — instead of only naming the state. The form performs the defense.

This is Performed Action applied to syntax rather than gesture: the sentence acts out the denial instead of reporting it.

Canon principle:

> If the character is protesting, let the sentence protest with them.

Named state (forbidden — overexplained psychology):

> Katlas didn't want to admit he was worried.

Rhetorical insistence:

> Katlas wasn't worried. He definitely wasn't worried. He checked the path four more times to prove it.

Named state (forbidden):

> Erlen pretended not to care that he'd been picked last.

Rhetorical insistence:

> Erlen did not care. He especially did not care in the specific, rehearsed way of someone who has practiced not caring.

Failure mode: insistence as verbal tic. The device gets reached for out of habit rather than earned by a genuine defensive beat, and repeats often enough per character to become a stylistic crutch instead of a performed moment.

### Rhetorical Insistence Test

1. Is the character genuinely defensive or evasive here, or is this a rhythm trick reaching for a character?
2. Does the repetition reveal something the character would not say directly? If the narrator ends up naming the emotion anyway, the device failed.
3. Would cutting the repetition flatten the psychology, or only shorten the paragraph?
4. Has this device already been used for this character recently?

## Narrative Orchestrator Rule

The orchestrator is the narrator's permanent musical identity and taste governor. It preserves the story's recognizable behavior across every focal lens, situation, rhythm, and emotional pressure.

Character lens may change the instrument. Situation may change the key. Attention may change the notes. Curiosity may change the order of revelation. Paragraph rhythm may change the tempo. The orchestrator keeps the whole arrangement recognizably *Humane*.

Even when the scene shifts from Katlas to the children, from joy to ritual, from ritual to danger, or from danger to wonder, the narrator should still:

- explain through sideways implication;
- avoid flat naming when the way something happens is the point;
- use comic precision without overexplaining the joke;
- let sentences answer one question and raise another;
- trust the reader to infer meaning;
- make details behave, collide, and pay off;
- let charged actions perform character, pressure, humor, danger, ritual, or emotion;
- keep the prose musical, not merely informative;
- preserve warmth, even when it becomes dry, grave, or frightening;
- keep language as simple as the moment allows.

The orchestrator is not Katlas. Katlas can color the narrator, but the orchestrator is older and larger than him. It is the reason a Katlas paragraph, a children's ritual paragraph, an Erlen paragraph, and a danger paragraph can all feel different while still belonging to the same book.

### Taste Rule

A sentence may be funny, poetic, strange, sharp, or sideways, but it must still belong to the scene.

A joke should not exist only because it is clever. A metaphor should not exist only because it is pretty. A detail should not exist only because it is accurate. Each must earn its place by revealing character, changing pressure, sharpening rhythm, deepening mood, or preparing payoff.

### World-Made Wink Rule

The narrator may sometimes wink toward the reader, but the wink must be made out of the world.

A Bronze Age joke should use Bronze Age material: copper, wool, smoke, mud, rope, teeth, wet dogs, bad weather, dull knives, borrowed tools, green flakes, bad trades, family shame, bitter roots, or unreliable paths.

Good:

> Like the mushroom had borrowed copper and returned advice.

Also possible, if the moment wants more world texture:

> Like the mushroom had paid him in low-quality copper.

Too far:

> Like the mushroom had participated in the collapse of Bronze Age trade networks.

That last version may be funny, but it belongs to the author's desk, not Katlas's morning.

### Short-Line Pressure Rule

A short paragraph must strike, turn, deepen, embarrass, reveal, silence, or land a joke.

Good:

> Still no Branth.

Good:

> Again.

Weak, unless plainness is the intended joke:

> He ate the mushroom.

If a short line only reports movement, it should probably be absorbed into a stronger sentence where the action can perform.

## Focal Lens Rule

The narrator does not have one flat attitude. It adapts to the person or group carrying the scene.

Focal lens is not impersonation. The narrator does not become a different book each time focus changes. The focal character changes what the narrator notices, mistrusts, jokes about, fears, misreads, lingers on, or refuses to say plainly.

The orchestrator remains above the lens.

Canon principle:

> The character colors the instrument. The orchestrator keeps the song.

| Focal Character | Narrative Texture | What This Lens Changes | Failure Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Katlas** | Dry, practical, suspicious, protective, emotionally evasive | Notices usefulness, danger, inconvenience, hidden care, bad signs, people pretending not to worry | Every paragraph becomes a joke; danger becomes safe; tenderness gets erased |
| **Children as group** | Bright, nervous, proud, loud, easily embarrassed, desperate to be seen as older | Notices noise, imitation, pride, small status battles, embarrassment, adult attention, ritual seriousness arriving too early | Children become cute background texture; chaos becomes a list |
| **Erlen** | Quick, curious, defensive, wonder-prone, hungry for respect | Notices possibility, unfairness, skill, weakness, discovery, how others measure him | Curiosity becomes exposition delivery; defensiveness becomes whining |
| **Ayka** | Sharp, analytic, self-forging, socially precise | Notices patterns, flaws, leverage, logic, contradictions, how people can be moved | Intelligence becomes coldness; analysis replaces emotion |
| **Arceis** | Muscular, proud, status-aware, physically confident | Notices strength, posture, challenge, rank, skill, visible failure | Confidence becomes empty arrogance; physicality becomes bluntness |
| **Wyll** | Sensorily alert, pressured, watchful, easily overloaded but perceptive | Notices sound, texture, small wrongness, crowd pressure, breath, rhythm, too-many-things-at-once | Sensory detail becomes clutter; overload becomes unreadable |
| **Idris** | Tactile, symbolic, inward, object-centered | Notices weight, shape, texture, charm, pattern, private meaning, objects as emotional anchors | Symbolism becomes opaque; inwardness becomes absence |
| **Alessia** | Self-possessed, socially aware, controlled, polished but not cold | Notices presentation, restraint, status, gesture, timing, who is watching whom | Polish becomes emotional distance; control becomes stiffness |

### Focal Action Examples

Flat:

> Katlas waited for Branth.

Katlas lens:

> Katlas gave the path one last chance to produce Branth.

Flat:

> The children walked to the gate.

Children-group lens:

> The children came in knots and bursts, each trying to look as if the gate had been waiting for them personally.

Flat:

> Erlen looked at the bow.

Erlen lens:

> Erlen looked at the bow and tried not to count all the ways it could embarrass him.

Flat:

> Wyll heard the crowd.

Wyll lens:

> Wyll heard the crowd in layers: sandals, breath, straps, laughter, one cough too close, one word too sharp.

Flat:

> Alessia stood still.

Alessia lens:

> Alessia stood still in the careful way some people shouted.

### Focal Lens Test

Before accepting a paragraph, ask:

1. Who is coloring the attention?
2. What would this lens notice first?
3. What would this lens avoid admitting?
4. What kind of detail performs under this lens?
5. How does the rhythm change?
6. What is this lens's danger?
7. Does the orchestrator still keep the prose recognizably *Humane*?

## Situational Filter Rule

The narrator adapts to the scene's emotional weather.

Focal character decides who colors the attention. Situation decides what kind of weather passes through that attention.

Katlas under morning pressure is not the same as Katlas under danger. Children under excitement are not the same as children under ritual. Erlen under wonder is not the same as Erlen under shame.

The narrator remains recognizably *Humane*, but rhythm, distance, image density, sensory focus, humor level, paragraph shape, and performed action change with the situation.

Canon principle:

> The character is the lens. The situation is the filter.

### Situational Behavior Table

| Situation | Narrator Behavior | Details That Perform | Rhythm Shape | Humor Level | Failure Mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Morning excitement** | Brighter, quicker, more communal; lets voices and movement fill the frame | Smoke, dogs, straps, food, missing belts, horses, chatter, packed bags | Medium motion with short comic cuts; controlled bustle | Moderate if lens supports it | Becomes generic happy village noise |
| **Celebration or anticipation** | Warm, abundant, socially textured; emphasizes preparation and shared pride | Food, cloth, ash, resin, hands, gifts, color, carried things | Flowing medium-long paragraphs with small human interruptions | Gentle, social | Becomes decorative festival catalogue |
| **Ritual** | Slower, tactile, weighted; reverent without becoming stiff | Ash, oil, skin, breath, stillness, uneven marks, watching adults | Long build-ups broken by short human reactions | Low; ordinary life may intrude softly | Becomes solemn exposition or fake sacredness |
| **First journey beyond walls** | Excited but edged with fear; the world feels larger than expected | Gate stones, wet grass, packs, path, mist, distant trees, adult hands letting go | Medium forward motion with short wonder/fear beats | Light, nervous | Becomes simple travel logistics |
| **Danger noticed** | Narrows attention; removes excess cleverness; makes small details loud | Breath, scent, snapped twig, silence, wrong birdcall, still grass, hand on weapon | Shorter sentences, controlled pauses, fewer ornaments | Very low; only character-specific dry edge | Danger feels safe or too clever |
| **Pursuit** | Compresses perception; prioritizes movement, impact, choice, and failing breath | Feet, mud, branches, lungs, blood, straps, slipping grip | Short and medium fragments; little room to breathe | Almost none | Action becomes choreography without fear |
| **Dread** | Makes the familiar wrong before explaining why | Too much silence, wrong warmth, missing sound, odd smell, animals refusing a place | Broken rhythm; sudden short lines; delayed explanation | None | Dread becomes vague darkness |
| **Wonder** | Opens scale; lets beauty and fear coexist | Sky, water, light, impossible motion, reflections, trembling hands, breath stopping | Longer sentences with short awe-struck anchors | Low, unless wonder is filtered through Katlas or children | Wonder becomes purple prose |
| **Grief or tenderness** | Softens without sentimentality; trusts small gestures | Hands, cloth, breath, weight, absence, someone fixing what no longer matters | Quiet medium sentences; short pain beats | Low or absent | Becomes melodrama or explanation of feelings |
| **Exhaustion** | Heavy, practical, reduced ornament; the body edits the world | Feet, straps, sweat, hunger, cold, ache, rubbing skin, dull tools | Slower medium sentences; blunt endings | Dry, sparse | Becomes boring fatigue report |
| **Comic pressure** | Lets inconvenience reveal care, social order, fear, or pride | Bad straps, wet dogs, borrowed knives, rude mushrooms, stubborn sacks, children overacting adulthood | Long setup to short cut; short joke to larger consequence | High but controlled | Becomes detached stand-up comedy |
| **Discovery / invention** | Quickens curiosity; lets cause and effect feel magical without becoming magic | Hands testing, stone, water, fire, failed attempts, sudden pattern, someone watching | Short observations expanding into longer realization | Bright, surprised | Becomes science lecture |
| **Threshold / uncanny** | Lets reality feel slightly misaligned; perception sharpens but does not overexplain | Repeated sounds, impossible stillness, light behaving wrong, delayed pain, mismatched memory | Uneven rhythm; beautiful line cut by wrong detail | Very low | Becomes abstract cosmic exposition |

### Situational Filtering

The same action changes depending on situation.

Flat action:

> He looked at the path.

Morning pressure:

> He gave the path one last chance to produce Branth.

Danger:

> He looked at the path because the birds had stopped using it.

Dread:

> He looked at the path and realized it had become too quiet to be empty.

Wonder:

> He looked at the path, and for a moment it seemed less like a way forward than a question the earth had forgotten to close.

Exhaustion:

> He looked at the path. It was still there. Rude of it.

Canon principle:

> The situation decides how much cleverness the moment can survive.

### Situational Filter Test

Before accepting a paragraph, ask:

1. What is the scene's emotional weather?
2. What does this situation make louder, stranger, heavier, funnier, or more dangerous?
3. Should the narrator widen, tighten, slow, cut, or breathe?
4. What details perform under this filter?
5. What kind of humor can survive here?
6. What would be too clever for this moment?
7. Does the rhythm match the pressure?
8. Does the passage still sound like *Humane*?

## The Detail Engine

The narrator of *Humane* loves details, but it does not narrate decorative accidents for their own sake.

A detail must earn narrative existence by doing work. It may create motion, reveal psychology, deepen culture, set up payoff, sharpen mood, build a running pattern, change social pressure, explain the world through behavior, or alter the reader's understanding of a character, place, ritual, or danger.

Canon principle:

> The narrator notices many things, but only narrates the things that earn a future.

But earning a future is not enough. The detail should also perform in the present.

A detail should not sit on the page like an object in a painting. It should move, interrupt, embarrass, comfort, threaten, mislead, bind, expose, tempt, resist, or change the pressure of the scene.

Flat detail:

> There was a mushroom in the grass.

Earned but still plain:

> Katlas found a mushroom in the grass and ate it.

More *Humane*:

> The mushroom did not help.

Better:

> He put it in his mouth with the grim patience of a man ending a debate no one else knew had started. Then he chewed. Slowly. Bitterly. Like the mushroom had paid him in low-quality copper.

The mushroom matters because it is not only food. It becomes Katlas's opponent, breakfast, risk test, joke, texture, and transition pressure.

### Interactive Detail

The narrator does not describe the world as a painting. It describes the world as a chain of small causes happening in real time.

A catalogue says:

> A dog barked, a child laughed, bracelets clicked, smoke rose.

*Humane* should make details collide.

A dropped fish skin should ruin a dog's discipline only if that accident pays: it reveals childhood interrupting ritual dignity, shows how seriously adults repair an ash mark, starts a running joke, sets up later dog behavior, or changes how the reader understands the group.

A detail should earn its place by doing at least one of these things:

- moving the scene;
- revealing character;
- changing social pressure;
- causing a reaction;
- carrying ritual meaning;
- foreshadowing danger;
- setting up a payoff;
- showing culture through behavior;
- making the reader feel the emotional weather;
- stimulating imagination without forcing explanation.

### Explanation Through Behavior

The narrator may explain, but explanation should be braided into action, reaction, and consequence.

Avoid:

> The ritual was important to the children because it marked their first step toward adulthood.

Prefer the mechanism:

> A young person tries not to move while ash is pressed to their brow. A parent fixes the mark as if the future might notice uneven lines. An older sibling smirks, then goes quiet when the ash touches.

The reader should understand importance because the environment behaves as if it matters.

### Detail Test

Before keeping a detail, ask:

1. Does it move, reveal, pressure, disturb, comfort, mislead, or pay off?
2. Does it interact with another detail, person, sound, object, or expectation?
3. Does it make the world explain itself by behaving?
4. Does it stimulate imagination instead of closing it?
5. Would the scene lose pressure, rhythm, humor, meaning, or memory if the detail disappeared?

If not, remove it, reduce it, or make it perform.

## Distance and Lens Movement

The narrator is allowed to get as close or as wide as the situation requires.

It is not trapped at one distance. Suspense may tighten into breath, scent, pause, sound, and wrong silence. Cheerful communal scenes may widen into the village. Ritual may move close to hands, ash, breath, oil, skin, and stillness. Wonder may open toward sky, water, light, and impossible scale.

Canon principle:

> The narrator's distance is not fixed. Its attention is situational.

But distance changes must not feel like neutral camera jumps. The scene must pull the narrator's attention.

Flat transition:

> Katlas looked at the village. The village was busy.

More *Humane*:

> Katlas looked toward the lower path, found no Branth, and found almost everything else.

The better version does not only widen the frame. It carries expectation, absence, irritation, humor, motion, and curiosity.

### Lens Movement as Music

The narrator can move through character lenses like a song moving through verses, refrains, motifs, breaks, and returns.

The narrator is the song. Character focus is the leading instrument. Situation is the key, tempo, pressure, rhythm, and emotional weather. The orchestrator is the conductor that makes the whole arrangement still sound like the same story.

A Chapter 0 pattern may work like:

| Pattern | Narrative Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Katlas lens: deadpan, practical, suspicious, protective |
| **B** | Children as group: excitement, pride, fear, ritual heart |
| **C** | Erlen: quick curiosity, defensive pride, hunger to be seen |
| **D** | Wyll: sensory pressure, watchfulness, fragile courage |
| **E** | Alessia: control, polish, self-possession, social precision |

Possible movement:

```text
A → B → B → A
Katlas → Children → Ritual children → Katlas
```

Then:

```text
C → D → E → A/D/E
Erlen → Wyll → Alessia → Katlas/Wyll/Alessia blend
```

The narrator may change lead instruments, but it must keep the same song.

### Transition Rule

A lens transition should not happen because the narrator decides to change focus. It should happen because something in the scene pulls the narrator's attention.

Each transition has four parts:

1. **Anchor** — something from the current lens: Katlas's gaze, mushroom, suspicion, mutter, waiting, or practical choice.
2. **Pressure** — a reason attention must move: Branth is absent, children are too loud, bowls appear, horses arrive, silence changes, danger interrupts.
3. **Handoff detail** — the active note that carries us across: whistle, bowl of ash, child mistake, missing belt, adult gesture, horse sneeze, gate movement.
4. **New lens settles** — the new mood begins controlling rhythm, detail, distance, and emotional color.

For Chapter 0, the A → B transition should be caused by Katlas giving up on waiting for Branth and calling the children. Katlas opens the motion, but the children's arrival should gradually steal the emotional center.

Canon principle:

> Lens shifts must be caused by active handoff details inside the scene; the narrator changes lead instruments only when the world gives it a note to follow.

### Transition Test

Before accepting a transition, ask:

1. What was the old lens holding?
2. What pressure makes the old lens insufficient?
3. What object, sound, gesture, mistake, or absence pulls attention across?
4. Does the new lens settle gradually enough to feel earned?
5. Does the rhythm change with the lens?
6. Does the transition perform character, pressure, humor, ritual, danger, or emotion?
7. Does it still sound like *Humane*?

If the answer is only "the narrator moved the camera," the transition is not ready.

## Dynamic Paragraph Composition

Paragraphs are musical units.

Their length should follow pressure, not habit. The narrator should not settle into polite symmetry unless the scene itself needs trance, ritual, exhaustion, ceremony, or calm.

A long paragraph is not automatically richer. A short paragraph is not automatically stronger. Alternation is not automatically musical.

The paragraph must match the scene's pulse.

Canon principle:

> Paragraph length is composition. Pressure chooses the shape.

### Paragraph Shapes

| Paragraph Shape | Best Use |
|---|---|
| **Long paragraph** | Village bustle, ritual build-up, wonder, social complexity, layered emotion, sweeping motion, abundance |
| **Medium paragraph** | Action, movement, work, observation, dialogue setup, practical scene progression |
| **Short paragraph** | Punchline, dread, shame, danger, realization, emotional cut, Katlas deadpan, silence |
| **One-line paragraph** | Impact beat, reversal, hard joke, fear, grief, revelation, refusal, breath |
| **Repeated short beats** | Tracking, counting, danger, panic, ritual steps, narrowing attention |
| **Long → short collapse** | A big thought cut down by reality |
| **Short → long expansion** | A tiny detail opening into meaning |
| **Alternating long/short** | Comedy, panic, emotional instability, child chaos, controlled dissonance |
| **Return changed** | A repeated image, joke, phrase, or lens coming back with new meaning |

### Controlled Dissonance Rule

*Humane* should not use one polite rhythm for every scene.

The prose may break symmetry, shift paragraph length, interrupt itself, move from humor to tenderness to threat, or cut a lyrical passage with an ugly practical detail, as long as the emotional logic is clear.

The reader should feel surprise, not confusion.

Canon principle:

> Controlled dissonance is allowed when every strange beat belongs.

### Short-Line Pressure

A short paragraph must strike, turn, deepen, embarrass, reveal, silence, or land a joke.

Good:

> Still no Branth.

Good:

> Again.

Good:

> Of course they did.

Weak, unless plainness is the intended effect:

> He ate the mushroom.

If a short line only reports movement, it should probably be absorbed into a stronger sentence where the action can perform.

### Dynamic Pattern Examples

A busy village may swell:

> Vahllalhag had already begun the day without asking his permission. Smoke lifted from the harths. Dogs slipped through legs with the false purpose of creatures hoping no one asked for proof. Horses stamped near the lower path, their backs loaded with wheat for tomorrow's ceremony. Adults moved between velths with bowls and cloaks and the tight faces of people trying to send children into the world without showing the world their hands were shaking.

Then cut:

> The children made that difficult.

A joke may breathe, then snap:

> He put it in his mouth with the grim patience of a man ending a debate no one else knew had started. Then he chewed. Slowly. Bitterly. Like the mushroom had paid him in low-quality copper.

A transition may hold a beat before release:

> He put two fingers to his mouth.

> Not yet a whistle.

> But close.

### Sentence Length Still Matters

| Sentence Length | Best Use |
|---|---|
| **Short** | Pain, shock, threat, shame, recognition, punchline |
| **Medium** | Movement, work, dialogue, pressure, practical action |
| **Long** | Wonder, ritual, landscape, complex social observation, abundance |

But sentence length is not the whole song. Paragraph shape, placement, silence, repetition, interruption, and return matter just as much.

### Rhythm Test

Before accepting a passage, ask:

1. Does the paragraph length match the scene's pressure?
2. Is the rhythm too symmetrical for the emotional moment?
3. Does a short line actually hit, or does it only report?
4. Does a long paragraph gather energy, or does it merely list?
5. Does the passage know when to breathe and when to cut?
6. Does the rhythm change when the lens or situation changes?
7. Does the music feel composed, not random?

If the paragraphing feels correct but emotionally flat, the scene may have notes without music.

## Humor Rule

Humor belongs to pressure.

The narrator may be funny, but the humor should feel squeezed from character, situation, social friction, material inconvenience, fear, embarrassment, tenderness, or ordinary life interrupting importance.

Humor should come from:

- character contradiction;
- social embarrassment;
- practical inconvenience;
- children overperforming courage;
- adults pretending not to worry;
- Katlas protecting tenderness with irritation;
- ritual seriousness colliding with ordinary life;
- objects behaving as if they have opinions;
- the world refusing to become as noble as people want it to be.

Canon principle:

> The narrator may smile at people, but it should not sneer at them.

### Pressure-Made Humor

A joke should not arrive because the narrator wants to be clever. It should arrive because the scene has created pressure and the sentence found the pressure's weakest rib.

Good:

> The mushroom did not help.

Good:

> Dogs slipped through legs with the false purpose of creatures hoping no one asked for proof.

Good:

> One boy claimed he had been ready for ages, which would have been more convincing if his auntie had not been holding his belt.

These jokes work because they reveal pressure: Katlas's irritation, village bustle, children performing adulthood, adults trying not to worry.

### Humor as Hidden Care

Katlas's humor often protects tenderness.

He may complain about children, mistrust behaving land, insult a mushroom, or give Branth's punctuality a private blessing that would make a priest wash his ears. But beneath the irritation, he still shows up before dawn, checks the path, watches the children, and carries the burden properly.

Canon principle:

> Katlas can make care sound like inconvenience. The reader should still feel the care.

### World-Made Humor

Humor should be made out of the world's own materials: copper, wool, ash, smoke, mud, rope, teeth, bitter roots, wet dogs, stubborn sacks, borrowed knives, bad weather, family shame, and people pretending not to be afraid.

Good:

> Like the mushroom had paid him in low-quality copper.

This works as a quiet historical wink, but the sentence still works for readers who miss the reference. To one reader, it recalls bad ancient copper. To another, it simply means Katlas feels cheated by breakfast. Both readings belong.

Too far:

> Like the mushroom had committed a trade violation known across Mesopotamia.

That joke may be funny, but it steps outside Katlas's morning.

### Humor Under Emotional Weather

The situation decides how much humor the moment can survive.

| Situation | Humor Behavior |
|---|---|
| **Morning bustle** | Social, practical, lively, allowed to bounce |
| **Children performing adulthood** | Warm embarrassment; never cruel |
| **Ritual** | Low, soft, ordinary-life intrusion only |
| **Danger** | Rare; dry or bitter only if character-specific |
| **Dread** | Almost none; humor may make dread worse, not smaller |
| **Wonder** | Low; humor may humanize awe but must not puncture it |
| **Grief** | Very low or absent; no mocking pain |
| **Exhaustion** | Dry, blunt, body-level humor |

### Joke Discipline

A scene should not become a row of punchlines.

If every paragraph ends in a joke, the narrator starts sounding needy. Humor should vary placement: sometimes setup, sometimes interruption, sometimes aftertaste, sometimes withheld.

Good pattern:

> long pressure build  
> small comic cut  
> return to consequence

Risky pattern:

> joke  
> joke  
> joke  
> joke  
> emotional moment that no longer has room to breathe

### Humor Failure Modes

Avoid:

- detached stand-up comedy;
- cruelty toward vulnerable characters;
- modern internet snark that breaks the ancient world;
- jokes that explain themselves;
- jokes that exist only to prove the author knows a fact;
- punchlines that make danger safe;
- humor that flattens ritual;
- cleverness that steals attention from the living scene.

### Humor Test

Before keeping a joke, ask:

1. What pressure produced it?
2. Whose lens colors it?
3. Does it reveal character, culture, fear, embarrassment, tenderness, or social friction?
4. Is it made from the world's materials?
5. Would the scene lose warmth, rhythm, or pressure without it?
6. Does the situation allow this much cleverness?
7. Does the joke smile, or does it sneer?
8. Does it still sound like *Humane*?

If the joke is clever but homeless, cut it or rebuild it from the scene.

## Character Lens Calibration

Character lens notes help the narrator adapt without losing the orchestrator.

These notes are not cages. They are calibration handles. A character may grow, contradict themselves, hide themselves, or change across the story. The narrator should track those changes without reducing the character to one trick.

Canon principle:

> A character lens is a pressure pattern, not a personality prison.

### Stable Lens vs Candidate Arc

Character notes should separate stable behavior from undecided development.

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Stable lens** | Current guiding texture for how the narrator notices through this character |
| **Candidate arc** | Possible development path awaiting architect approval |
| **Failure mode** | The predictable version the character must avoid becoming |

The architect may approve, reject, or revise candidate arcs later.

### Expandable Character Lens Slots

This section may grow as the cast grows.

Not every character needs the same amount of calibration at first. A major character may need stable lens, candidate arc, failure mode, performed-action examples, and situational behavior. A smaller character may only need a short pressure pattern until the story asks for more.

Canon principle:

> Add character lens detail only when the story needs the narrator to behave differently around that character.

Each expandable character slot may use:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Stable lens** | What currently guides the narrator through this character |
| **Narrative pressure** | What emotional, social, physical, or thematic force this character brings |
| **What this lens notices** | The details this character makes louder |
| **Performed-action tendency** | How actions change under this lens |
| **Candidate arc** | Possible development awaiting architect approval |
| **Failure mode** | The predictable version to avoid |
| **Canon status** | Locked, draft, candidate, or watchlist |

### Character Availability Gate

A character may have a calibrated lens before entering the story, but the narrator should not use that lens inside scene prose until the character is physically present, remembered, expected, or meaningfully implied by the scene.

Canon principle:

> A lens may exist in the engine before it exists in the reader's world.

Use a character lens when:

- the character is physically present;
- the scene is close to that character's perception;
- another character is actively thinking about them;
- the character's absence is dramatically meaningful;
- the story has introduced enough context for the reader to understand the lens.

Do not use a character lens when:

- the character has not been introduced;
- the scene does not need them;
- the lens would spoil future emotional territory;
- the narrator is borrowing a useful texture from the wrong person.

Failure mode:

> The narrator borrows a future character's flavor too early and makes the world feel wider in the wrong direction.

Example:

Muyu's "care under fire" lens may exist in canon, but Chapter 0 should not use Muyu-like triage attention before Muyu enters or becomes relevant. If a scene needs care, injury, or urgency before Muyu appears, those details must be filtered through the present character lens instead: Katlas, Erlen, Wyll, Alessia, Arceis, or the children as a group.

### Character Lens Interface Rule

Each major character file may contain a **Narrative Lens Interface**. This section defines how the narrator behaves when the prose scopes close to that character.

The Narrative Lens Interface does not replace the narrator. It colors the narrator.

Canon principle:

> The character colors the instrument. The orchestrator keeps the song.

When writing close to a character, consult that character's Narrative Lens Interface before generating prose.

A Narrative Lens Interface may define:

- lens activation;
- dominant lens energy;
- what the lens notices first;
- prose behavior when scoped in;
- syntax and rhythm;
- humor and rhetorical energy;
- emotional defense;
- stress mode;
- dialogue-prose boundary;
- sample lens lines;
- failure modes.

The narrator should remain recognizably *Humane*, but the character should change the pressure of the sentence.

Failure mode:

> The narrator either ignores the character lens completely, making everyone sound the same, or becomes the character too fully and loses the orchestrator.

### Katlas Calibration

Katlas may color the narration with deadpan, but he should not take over the narrator.

Stable lens:

- dry;
- practical;
- suspicious;
- protective;
- emotionally evasive;
- more useful than he wants people to notice;
- funny because he treats tenderness like a logistical inconvenience.

Katlas humor should feel like practical pessimism leaking into the prose. He may insult a mushroom, mistrust behaving land, or complain around responsibility, but the reader should still feel that he showed up before dawn and is doing the work properly.

Canon principle:

> Katlas can make care sound like inconvenience. The reader should still feel the care.

Allowed:

- dry timing;
- suspicious observations;
- practical contradiction;
- underplayed emotional weight;
- humor that protects tenderness rather than erasing it;
- physical signs of irritation, tiredness, competence, or restraint.

Risky:

- every paragraph ending in a joke;
- humor that makes danger safe;
- making Katlas the narrator instead of a narrator lens;
- modern jokes that feel external to his morning;
- reducing him to "grumpy mentor";
- making tenderness invisible for too long.

#### Katlas Drinking Candidate

Katlas may have a drinking-related character arc, but this is not locked canon until the architect approves it.

Possible arc:

> Katlas drinks early in the story as part of avoidance, grief, habit, pain, social role, or self-punishment, then gradually stops as responsibility, attachment, and purpose become harder to dodge.

If used, drinking should not become his whole personality or an easy joke machine. It should alter behavior, rhythm, body language, reliability, shame, irritability, tenderness, and how others read him.

Show through behavior before naming:

- slower hands when he wants them steady;
- breath someone notices and chooses not to mention;
- a flask handled like a tool, not a party;
- irritation sharpened by shame;
- competence that survives the habit, making the habit more painful rather than funny;
- moments where not drinking becomes a choice the reader can feel.

Failure mode:

> Katlas becomes "the drunk funny mentor" instead of a wounded, useful, evasive man with a possible drinking problem.

Canon status:

> Candidate. Do not hard-code until approved.

### Arceis Calibration

Arceis should not become a predictable brooding rival.

His current base is physical confidence, pride, status awareness, and skill, but the direction should lean away from cold anime-rival gloom and toward dominant athlete energy: blunt, physical, confident, socially visible, a little absurd in how naturally the body obeys him.

Use "Haaland vibes" as energy, not imitation.

Stable lens:

- physically gifted;
- direct;
- proud;
- competitive;
- status-aware;
- socially loud without needing many words;
- intimidating because he seems built for the task in front of him;
- not mysterious for mystery's sake.

Arceis should feel like someone whose confidence has evidence behind it.

He does not need to brood in corners. He can smile. He can joke. He can be blunt. He can be annoying because he is often right about physical things. His danger is not darkness. His danger is momentum.

Possible narrative texture:

- notices posture, speed, grip, distance, strength, balance, ranking;
- measures people through visible action;
- trusts bodies faster than words;
- enjoys challenge because challenge confirms the world is arranged correctly;
- can embarrass Erlen without intending to sound poetic about it.

Good Arceis direction:

> Not "I am lonely and superior."  
> More "I am obviously good at this. Why is everyone making it complicated?"

Failure modes:

- Sasuke-shaped brooding rival;
- generic bully;
- empty arrogance without competence;
- beautiful danger with no social texture;
- rivalry that exists only to make Erlen insecure.

Canon principle:

> Arceis should feel physically inevitable before he feels emotionally complicated.

### Muyu Calibration

Use "Ajay Che / Lifeline vibes" as energy, not imitation.

Muyu's lens should carry bright pressure, practical care, moral bite, and battlefield awareness. She is not gentle because the world is gentle. She is gentle because someone has to be, and she may be very annoyed about that.

Stable lens:

- fast under pressure;
- caring with teeth;
- funny when fear needs somewhere to go;
- practical before she is poetic;
- allergic to useless panic;
- drawn toward injury, weakness, and urgent need;
- morally aware of violence without becoming helpless before it;
- dangerous because her body moves toward the right priority before others understand the problem.

Narrative pressure:

> Muyu enters chaos and immediately begins sorting it: who is hurt, who is lying, who is pretending to be fine, who is about to make things worse, and what can still be saved.

What this lens notices:

- breath changing;
- blood hidden badly;
- shaking hands;
- uneven steps;
- bad bandages;
- empty water skins;
- people pretending not to hurt;
- pride getting in the way of survival;
- the one person nobody is watching;
- the shortest path between danger and someone who needs help.

Performed-action tendency:

> Muyu does not simply react to danger. She triages it.

Flat:

> Muyu helped him stand.

More *Humane*:

> Muyu got under his arm before his pride finished objecting.

Flat:

> Muyu noticed he was hurt.

More *Humane*:

> Muyu saw the lie in his breathing before she saw the blood.

Flat:

> Muyu ran toward the injured child.

More *Humane*:

> Muyu was moving before anyone had decided whether screaming counted as a plan.

Humor tendency:

Muyu's humor should be quick, teasing, and pressure-made. She may sound light because lightness keeps people from freezing.

Good:

> "Hold still," Muyu said, which was generous, because he had not been doing anything useful with movement either.

Good:

> She tied the bandage tighter than his dignity preferred.

Failure modes:

- generic healer girl;
- saint with no bite;
- quip machine;
- support character who only exists to help others develop;
- combat medic who treats violence as clean;
- cheerful character whose cheer erases moral pressure.

Canon principle:

> Muyu should feel like care under fire: bright enough to keep people moving, sharp enough to cut through panic, and human enough to hate that this is necessary.

Canon status:

> Draft calibration. Awaiting architect approval.

### Character Calibration Test

Before accepting a character-heavy paragraph, ask:

1. Is this character coloring the attention, or hijacking the whole narrator?
2. What does this character notice that others would miss?
3. What does this character avoid admitting?
4. What kind of detail performs under this lens?
5. What is the character's current stable lens?
6. Is this beat using an approved canon trait or an undecided candidate arc?
7. What predictable version of this character must be avoided?
8. Does the orchestrator still keep the prose recognizably *Humane*?

## Question-Answer Chain Rule

The narrator should stimulate the reader's mind without insulting it.

Each important sentence should try to do at least two of the following:

- answer a small reader question;
- raise a slightly deeper question;
- reveal character indirectly;
- imply time, place, role, danger, relationship, or social pressure;
- preserve curiosity instead of closing it too early;
- leave a clue, contradiction, or emotional residue.

Canon principle:

> The narrator reveals character by answering one small question while quietly creating the next.

This is why Katlas's opening works.

| Sentence Function | What It Gives | What It Raises |
|---|---|---|
| Katlas is awake before sunrise and has already decided mistakes will happen | Name, time, deadpan expectation, responsibility | Who are they? What mistakes? Why is he in charge? |
| He supposedly lost a bet and showed up with a knife and an eye-roll | Task, social rumor, practical world, children, reluctance | Did he really lose? Why children? Why again? |
| He does not whine in front of them and acts like he volunteered | Hidden care, dignity, protective performance | How much does he actually care? What happened before? |

The narrator should not say:

> Katlas was sarcastic, responsible, and secretly protective.

It should leave intelligent footprints:

> He arrives before dawn. He denies the bet without denying the duty. He complains around the children, but not in front of them. He acts like the burden is accidental while carrying it properly.

## Children and Ritual Rule

Chapter 0 opens through Katlas, but it does not belong only to Katlas.

Katlas is the handhold. The children are the heart. The Ereenhaad ritual is the first major emotional transfer of the chapter.

Once the children enter, the narrator should widen and brighten. Their excitement, fear, pride, embarrassment, and longing to be seen should alter the voice. The children should not remain background texture watched by Katlas. The narrator must let their threshold matter.

Canon principle:

> Katlas opens the door. The children bring the heart. The ritual tells the reader what the world values before the journey tests whether those values survive.

The shift should happen after the opening has made the reader comfortable with Katlas, around the moment Katlas stops waiting for Branth and calls the children to the gate.

## Chapter 0 A → B Application

The long A section introduces Katlas, his deadpan, his competence, his suspicion, and his hidden care. It should not be cut too early, because Katlas is the reader's first handhold.

The B section begins when Katlas decides waiting for Branth has become pointless and calls the children.

The children should gather with mothers, fathers, grandparents, aunties, uncles, older siblings, teachers, neighbours, or whatever responsible adult has claimed them for the morning. This should not yet complete the ritual. It should build toward it.

The narration should make the transition feel like this:

```text
A: Katlas waits, scans, mistrusts good signs, mutters about Branth.
Handoff: Katlas decides not to wait and calls the children.
B begins: Vahllalhag starts producing children and responsible adults toward the gate.
B deepens: the bowls of ash and resin approach; excitement becomes quieter because the ritual is near.
Next section: Ereenhaad marking.
```

The key is that Katlas causes the transition, but the children must take emotional ownership of it.

## Pre-Star-Piece Knowledge Rule

Before the Star Piece, the narrator should remain human-limited. It may be perceptive, poetic, comic, and emotionally sharp, but it should not explain modern science, cosmic systems, or future mechanics in direct technical language.

After the Star Piece, perception may become more precise and strange, but the narrator should not lose warmth or mystery.

## Architect Rule

The architect is not another narrator mechanism inside the prose. The architect is the authorial judge above the system.

The narrator performs the song. The orchestrator keeps the song sounding like *Humane*. The architect decides which song becomes canon.

Canon is not the version that obeys every rule. Canon is the version that best serves the soul, direction, and memory of the story.

The architect may break any rule when the living passage demands it.

## Practical Scene Calibration Rubric

Before writing or revising a scene, answer:

| Calibration Question | Example for Chapter 0 A → B |
|---|---|
| Who colors the current lens? | Katlas, then children as group |
| What is the situation? | Morning, anticipation, first journey, ritual build-up |
| What distance is needed? | Katlas-close, then widening village movement, then ritual-near |
| What details earn narration? | Branth's absence, Katlas's call, adults claiming children, bowls of ash/resin, children trying to look ready |
| Which actions need to perform? | Waiting, eating the mushroom, looking down the path, calling the children, noticing the bowls |
| What does each sentence answer or raise? | Why stop waiting? Who answers the call? Why does the gathering matter? Why do the bowls change the mood? |
| What preserves the orchestrator? | Sideways implication, active detail, performed action, comic precision, no neutral report prose |
| How simple should the language be? | As simple as possible while keeping the sentence alive |
| What should the reader feel? | Katlas's impatience, children's excitement, adult tenderness, ritual anticipation, faint threat-shadow |
| What must not happen? | A flat list of children, adults, and objects; neutral exposition; immediate Branth arrival before the children breathe; difficult language used as decoration |

## Working Aphorisms

- Character is the lens. Situation is the filter. The orchestrator is the camera that remembers the whole film.
- The goal is not to label emotion. The goal is to let the prose behave as if the emotion has changed the light.
- Katlas opens the door. The children bring the heart.
- The narrator changes instruments, not songs.
- Detail is not decoration. Detail is evidence.
- Details do not merely earn narration. Details perform.
- The narrator notices many things, but only narrates the things that earn a future.
- A sentence should answer one question and raise a better one.
- Do not say the thing happened if the way it happened is the point.
- The world should explain itself by behaving.
- Humor belongs to pressure.
- A joke may wink at the reader, but it must still be made out of the world.
- Paragraph length is composition. Pressure chooses the shape.
- Controlled dissonance is allowed when every strange beat belongs.
- A character lens is a pressure pattern, not a personality prison.
- The narrator may be modern in rhythm, but ancient in texture.
- The orchestrator keeps the soul when the lens changes.
- Simple words can carry deep water.
- The prose should be easy to enter and hard to empty.
- No matter what happens, still sound like *Humane*.
